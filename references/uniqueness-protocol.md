# Uniqueness protocol

This document is mandatory for every website project using `unslop-business`.

## Purpose

Prevent generic, interchangeable websites and repeated design outputs. The goal is not random novelty. The goal is a coherent visual and structural identity derived from the actual business.

## Step 1 — Build a business fingerprint

Write down:
- Business category and subcategory.
- Audience and buying/visiting context.
- Main offer and differentiators.
- Brand personality (3–5 precise adjectives).
- Primary conversion.
- Content and assets available.
- Market expectations and practical constraints.

Do not use generic descriptors alone. Explain what "premium" means for this specific business.

## Step 2 — Make a design fingerprint

Complete this before coding:

```text
Project:
Business:
Audience:
Primary conversion:

Creative concept:
Visual mood:
Hero composition:
Typography direction:
Color logic:
Image/art direction:
Grid/layout strategy:
Section/story sequence:
Navigation behavior:
Interaction/motion signature:
Distinctive detail:
Patterns intentionally avoided:
Reference principles used:
Original interpretation:
```

The choices must be concrete and mutually coherent.

## Step 3 — Choose a composition, not a template

Choose the layout based on the content and visitor task. Possible composition families include:
- Editorial asymmetric split.
- Full-bleed image with restrained overlay.
- Product-first catalog landing.
- Story-led vertical narrative.
- Location/experience-led discovery.
- Typographic manifesto.
- Service/problem-solution architecture.
- Campaign-led modular composition.
- Utility-first local business layout.

These are examples, not a preset menu to cycle through. Invent a composition when the brief calls for it. Do not use the same family for every project.

## Step 4 — Enforce meaningful variation

Vary several major design dimensions when building a different business or a new project:
- Hero geometry and alignment.
- Content density and page rhythm.
- Typography character and hierarchy.
- Image treatment and aspect ratios.
- Section sequence.
- Navigation model.
- Product/service presentation.
- CTA placement and behavior.
- Surface, border, and shape language.
- Motion approach.

Do not create variation by changing only colors, fonts, wording, or images. At least the composition, information architecture, and content presentation should be reconsidered.

## Step 5 — Check for duplication

If another project is visible in the current workspace, conversation, or user-provided examples:
1. Compare its design fingerprint with this one.
2. List the repeated patterns.
3. Decide which are necessary conventions and which are accidental repetition.
4. Change accidental repetition in this project.

If no previous work is visible, do not pretend to have compared against it. Do not claim access to designs generated for other users. An agent without shared persistent storage cannot guarantee uniqueness across independent users or sessions.

## Step 6 — The logo-swap test

Ask:

"If I replace the logo, text, and photos with those of another unrelated business, would this design still look almost exactly right?"

If yes, it is too generic. Rework:
- The main composition.
- The content hierarchy.
- The section order.
- The visual storytelling.
- The product/service presentation.

## Step 7 — Final uniqueness review

A successful design should:
- Clearly belong to the business.
- Reflect its actual audience and offer.
- Have a recognizable visual signature.
- Use a distinct page composition.
- Avoid obvious AI-template clichés.
- Be original relative to supplied references.
- Remain usable, accessible, responsive, and commercially effective.

Uniqueness is a design discipline, not a claim of mathematically guaranteed global originality.
